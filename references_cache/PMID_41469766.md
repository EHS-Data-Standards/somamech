# Seasonal impact of PM(2.5) on pro-oncogenic drivers among participants of a thermal power plant in India.
**Authors:** Ghosh S, Chaudhuri R, Mukherjee M, Henneman L, Saha P, Samanta A, Dey S, Sengupta MR, Sinha D
**Journal:** Sci Rep (2025)
**DOI:** [10.1038/s41598-025-33906-7](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-33906-7)

## Content

Molecular epidemiology targeting the impact of particulate matter ≤ 2.5 μm in aerodynamic diameter (PM2.5) on pro-oncogenic signaling has been sparsely reported from the Indian industrial belt. This study investigated the effect of seasonal variation in PM2.5 on pro-oncogenic drivers in the pulmonary and systemic cellular environments of a thermal power plant participants in West Bengal, India during 2021-2022. PM2.5 levels were significantly higher during spring than monsoon, coinciding with reduced hemoglobin and lung function indices. Linear Mixed-Effects Regression (LMER) confirmed that elevated PM2.5 exposure was significantly associated with increased inflammatory response, enhanced matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) activity, and upregulation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)/phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT) cascade, along with downregulation of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN). These molecular alterations reflected enhanced proliferative and pro-angiogenic signaling and reduced apoptosis in the airway cells and the leukocytes. Collectively, the findings indicate that short-term seasonal surges in PM2.5, may induce subclinical molecular perturbations that predispose exposed individuals in industrial areas to activation of the EGFR/PI3K/AKT pro-oncogenic signaling.

771. Introduction
78Ambient air pollution is a global environmental crisis and India has 
79been confronting a severe deterioration in air quality throughout the last 
80decade 1. Outdoor air pollution is primarily driven by the air pollutant 
81particulate matter ≤2.5 µm in aerodynamic diameter (PM 2.5) that is a 
82mixture of several components including nitrates, sulphates, polycyclic 
83aromatic hydrocarbons, heavy metals, volatile organic compounds, 
84bioaerosols, and other air toxicants 2,3,4. According to the 2024 World Air 
85Quality Report, India ranked fifth among 134 countries in PM 2.5 pollution, 
86and New Delhi was one of the worst-performing regional capitals—the 
87population-weighted annual mean PM 2.5 was 50.6 µg/m³ for India and 91.6 
88µg/m³ for New Delhi 5. Nearly 1.36 billion people in India are exposed to 
89annual PM 2.5 levels that far exceed the World Health Organization (WHO) 
90guideline limit of 5 µg/m³  5. A thorough analysis by the Central Pollution 
91Control Board Continuous Ambient Air Quality Monitoring network, the 
92National Air Quality Monitoring Program and the US Air–Now network 
93between 2015–2019 manifested that in north and south India the annual 
94average PM 2.5 surpassed the National Ambient Air Quality Standards 
95(NAAQS) guideline (40 µg/m 3) by 100% and 40% respectively 6. The Indian 
96industrial belt serves as one of the major contributors of ambient PM 2.5 
97emissions 7.
98PM 2.5 is attributed to five major diseases: ischemic heart disease, 
99chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, stroke, diabetes and lung cancer

100 8 and associated premature mortality 9.  Lung cancers are the deadliest of 
101all cancers with poor prognosis and a low 5-year survival rate of 26.7% 10. 
102PM 2.5 has been repeatedly associated with lung cancer mortality 11,12,13. 
103PM 2.5 being small in size penetrates the broncho-mucosal layer and settles 
104in the alveoli. Inside the pulmonary environment, PM 2.5 is responsible for 
105repetitive injury leading to inflammation, generation of reactive oxygen 
106species (ROS), induction of genetic mutations and activation of several 
107pro-oncogenic pathways leading to lung cancers 14. The phosphoinositide 
1083-kinase (PI3K)/protein kinase B (AKT)/mammalian target of rapamycin 
109(mTOR) signaling pathway has been frequently reported with the 
110progression of lung cancer 15. The PI3Ks are intracellular lipid kinases 
111which are activated by binding of growth factors/cytokines/hormones to 
112their G- protein coupled receptors or receptor tyrosine kinases. PI3K in 
113turn phosphorylates AKT and mTOR resulting in the transactivation of 
114several cancer-progressive genes which inhibit cell death, induce actin 
115reorganization and influence cell motility 16,17. 
116Industrial areas of India are hotspots of air pollution. PM 2.5-
117attributed lung cancer risk in India is well documented 18,19 but the 
118mechanistic underpinnings of the same are not well elucidated. The impact 
119of seasonal variation of PM 2.5 on pro-oncogenic molecular epidemiology in 
120Indian industrial backdrop has been sparsely reported. Therefore, it was 
121imperative to study the effect of PM 2.5 variation during different seasons 
122(monsoon and spring) on the pro-oncogenic signaling in a thermal power 
123plant in West Bengal, India. The study focused on paired seasonal samples 
124from participants to gain an in-depth understanding of how PM 2.5 
125influences pro-oncogenic cues involving the epidermal growth factor 
126receptor (EGFR)/PI3K/AKT pathway within the pulmonary and systemic 
127cellular environments of individuals exposed at the thermal power plant.

352 2.8. Relative risk (RR) of lung cancer mortality due to PM 2.5
353The long-term estimated risk for lung cancer mortality for the power 
354plant participants (with X 0, 5 μg/m3) during winter-spring [RR, 1.606; 95% 
355CI, 1.191, 2.167] was higher than summer-monsoon [RR, 1.384; 95% CI, 
3561.127, 1.701]. The long-term risk of lung cancer mortality increased by 
35760.6% during winter-spring and by 38.4 % during summer-monsoon 
358compared to populations who are exposed to the WHO recommended 
359annual average level of PM 2.5 (5 μg/m3).  A similar tendency was observed

3693. Discussion
370Air pollutant PM 2.5 is a major public health concern across the globe. 
371During the last decade, India has frequently witnessed an alarming surge 
372in poor air quality especially during the winter season 24. The average mass 
373concentration of PM 2.5 over the middle Indo–Gangetic plain and Delhi is 
374much higher compared to the lower and upper Gangetic plains thereby 
375increasing the lung cancer risks of the residents in these areas 25. 
376Industrial emission of PM 2.5 is one of the primary contributors of 
377deterioration of air quality index in India 7, 26. PM2.5 exerts its toxic effects 
378by penetrating deep into the airways and settling in the alveoli. Lung 
379cancer risk associated with PM 2.5 has been reported from India 18, 19 but 
380the mechanistic underpinnings of the same are yet to be deciphered. 
381Moreover, molecular epidemiology targeting the impact of PM 2.5 on pro-
382oncogenic signaling has been sparsely reported from the Indian industrial 
383belt. In this study, we tried to investigate the effect of seasonal variation 
384in PM 2.5 on the pro-oncogenic signaling- PI3K/AKT network of the 
385participants of a thermal power plant in West Bengal, India. The biological 
386samples were collected twice from the same asymptomatic males - once 
387post-summer, during monsoon months and the other after winter during 
388spring. It was hypothesized that the PM 2.5-induced changes in the 
389biological parameters, if any, may be observed through the comparative 
390study of the monsoon samples (post low PM 2.5 summer exposure) and the 
391spring samples (post high PM 2.5 winter exposure). We observed that there 
392was a distinct surge of PM 2.5 in the locality of the thermal power plant 
393during the period Dec 2021-Mar 2022 (winter-spring) than the period Apr 
3942021-Aug 2021 (summer-monsoon) which was validated by both

396online data repository.
397The study enrolled healthy male participants without any chronic 
398diseases or any form of addiction. Their general health survey revealed 
399that the hematological parameters during both monsoon and spring were 
400within normal limits. However, compared with the monsoon season, 
401participants in spring showed reduced Hb, ESR, and WBC levels, 
402indicating a shift toward anemia. In concordance with our findings, recent 
403studies have proposed that ambient PM 2.5 may be associated with 
404decreased Hb levels and anaemia in pregnant women 27 and children 28.  A 
405Chinese study reported that PM 2.5 may be attributed to a decrease in WBC, 
406increase in red blood corpuscle (RBC) and thrombocyte counts in women 
407planning for pregnancy 29. The decline in Hb and ESR during spring 
408suggested an anemic tendency, while the combination of low WBC counts 
409and elevated monocytes pointed to immune modulation and an altered 
410hematopoietic profile, potentially reflecting systemic stress or 
411inflammatory activation. 
412Apart from hematology, the pulmonary health of the participants was 
413also checked using pulmonary function test (PFT) and sputum cytology. A 
414systematic review reported that short-term PM 2.5 exposure has been often 
415associated with reduced lung function in children from Brazil, China and 
416Japan 30.  On a similar note, we observed that with increase in PM 2.5 
417exposure during spring, the power plant participants showed reduced 
418FEV1/FVC which indicated a tendency toward the development of 
419obstructive lung function. PM 2.5 exposure has been also related to 
420compromised host immune response and susceptibility to respiratory 
421infections 31. In convergence with this, during spring, the individuals were

423primary cells recruited against any respiratory pathogen. Decrease in 
424alveolar macrophages has been associated with neutrophil infiltration,

426Similarly, in the present study the pulmonary environment of the 
427participants during spring exhibited an increased tendency of neutrophil

430Aberrant activation of PI3K/AKT/mTOR has been frequently

792 4.13. Statistical analysis
793Descriptive statistics were used to explore the data using Student’s 
794t-test. P-value  < 0.05 indicated a statistically significant difference. 
795Transcript and protein expression levels of the study participants (N, 33) 
796obtained from RT-qPCR and ELISA respectively during the two seasons 
797(monsoon and spring) were compared using paired t-test. In order to 
798reduce the incidence of false positives, Bonferroni’s method of adjustment 
799of p-values for family-wise error rate control was used, setting α at 0.05. 
800Correlation analyses between ROS and MMP-9 were done with Pearson’s 
801correlation test. The LMER model was used to determine the effect of 
802PM 2.5 exposure level on the significantly altered parameters, namely, 
803hematology, PFT, sputum cytology, as well as differentially expressed 
804genes and proteins in the pulmonary and systemic cellular environment, 
805while adjusting for demographic and socioeconomic variables. The 
806covariates of the model were age, BMI, marital status, number of family 
807members, education, profession, income, number of working years, 
808duration of work, type of transport used for going to the workplace, 
809exposure to passive smoking and type of cooking fuel. Since majority of 
810the participants did not test for COVID-19 infection, we could not include 
811this covariate in the LMER model. Identity codes of the participants were 
812set as the random variable of interest in the LMER model. To examine 
813whether PM 2.5 exposure was correlated with all the above-mentioned 
814covariates included in the LMER model, we conducted a linear regression 
815with PM 2.5 concentration as the dependent variable and all covariates as 
816independent predictors. Multicollinearity was assessed using VIF with a
